How cases get found, sourced, graded and kept current

Where the data stands right now

Every number on this page is computed live from the dataset the tracker itself runs on (data/usecases.json) — so it can't drift out of date as new cases are added.

The approach in one paragraph

The tracker records concrete, named things banks and payment companies are actually doing with AI — a deployment, a pilot, a partnership, a measurable result. It deliberately does not try to be a news feed about AI in finance. Vendor launches, analyst predictions, regulatory consultations and executive commentary are all interesting, but they are not use cases, and mixing them in would quietly inflate the counts. Those belong in the Industry benchmarks & reports panel instead, which is curated separately.

What gets in, what doesn't

Included

  • A named bank or payment company deploying, piloting or announcing a specific AI capability.
  • Cases announced by a technology partner (Microsoft, Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS, NVIDIA, FIS, Fiserv…) — the record is created for the bank, with the partner recorded in the vendor field.
  • Card networks, processors, acquirers, BNPL and payment fintechs, alongside banks.
  • Events dated 1 August 2023 onwards, without exception.
  • Cases framed around the business improvement — what got faster, cheaper, safer or better.

Excluded

  • Pure technology vendors, consultancies and credit bureaus that aren't themselves a bank or payment system.
  • Reports, surveys and predictions — these go to the benchmarks panel.
  • Regulatory actions and central-bank commentary — context, not deployments.
  • Personnel moves, hiring and M&A, unless the substance is an AI capability.
  • Anything without a working source URL. Nothing is ever recorded from memory or inference.
  • Duplicate coverage of an event already in the dataset.

How sources are graded

Every record carries a source tier (how authoritative the source is) and a confidence rating (how much weight the claim can bear). Weaker sourcing is never hidden or dressed up — a case backed only by an aggregator is kept and labelled honestly, so the mix stays visible.

Preference order when several sources cover the same event: the institution's own newsroom → the technology partner's press release → established trade or business press → everything else. When a stronger source appears later, the record is upgraded.

How cases get found

Publishers are one thing; the route by which a case surfaces is another. Award programmes, institutions' own newsrooms and vendor case studies each catch different deployments, so the tracker records the channel behind every case. A healthy mix matters — leaning on any single channel would quietly shape which banks and regions get represented.

How often it updates

An automated research task runs every morning. It searches for announcements from roughly the previous week, applies the rules above, de-duplicates against every existing record by both ID and source URL, and commits the result. The published site redeploys automatically.

On days when nothing new clears the bar, the task still writes a heartbeat recording that it ran and found nothing. That's deliberate: silence should never be ambiguous between "no news today" and "the pipeline is broken."

Honest limitations

  • English-language bias. Coverage of institutions that publish mainly in other languages is thinner than their actual AI activity warrants.
  • Announcement bias. The dataset records what organisations choose to make public. Banks that deploy quietly are under-represented, and announced ≠ delivered.
  • Self-reported metrics. Outcome figures come from the institutions themselves and are rarely independently audited. Treat them as claims, not findings.
  • Uneven depth. Large, communicative institutions accumulate more records — that reflects publishing behaviour as much as it reflects AI maturity.
  • A curated view, not a census. Search has finite reach; this is a well-sourced sample of the market, not a complete register of it.
  • History is landmark-weighted. The August 2023 – December 2025 records were backfilled retrospectively, so they favour deployments that were notable enough to still be findable. Recent months are tracked continuously and are therefore denser. A rising case count over time reflects both real growth in activity and this difference in method.
  • Programmes get revised. Some early deployments were later scaled back or rebalanced toward human staff. Where a reversal is well sourced it is recorded as its own dated entry, so a case from 2024 should not be read as the last word on that programme.

Every record links to its source. Anything that matters should be confirmed there before it's relied on.
